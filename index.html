    A little help?

    Pick a topic. The kitchen waits while you read.

    Free practice

    is the same simulation with no tickets or scoring. Add up to four patties, discard food or clear the bench and experiment. Pause freezes cooking; this Help window does too. Save stores your kitchen in this browser, with separate service and practice slots. Active sessions autosave every 15 seconds. Saved sessions restore paused, without offline cooking; check the save status before closing.

    Requested builds

    appear on the order slip. Click the ticket to expand it. Each burger needs its listed ingredients; a missing ingredient sends it back. A check mark means assigned, not cooked. Back on the heat reheats resting food without erasing its history. Discard removes a topping so you can cook a replacement; bun halves are discarded and assigned together.

    Viewport

    left-drag orbits (unless you start on a patty, which drags it across the pan instead), wheel or right-drag zooms, right-click zooms onto whatever you clicked (right-click again to back out), middle/shift-drag pans. Keys 1–4 are view presets, R resets, arrows orbit. Cutaway (C) slices the patty so you can watch the grey band creep inward.

    Physics

    the patty is a grid of ~0.6 mm layers by ~4 mm rings, so the rim and the middle are different places. Each cell has its own temperature, water, fat and denatured-protein state. Heat comes in through a contact conductance from the pan (twelve rings of metal with a burner hot spot), water boils off at 100 °C and pins the surface there until it dries, juice squeezed out by contracting proteins migrates to the faces, fat melts and drains, and once the surface is dry and hot the Maillard reaction browns it — then pyrolysis blackens it. Colour, shine, steam, smoke, spatter and sizzle are all driven by those numbers. The whole thing steps every 0.05 s of simulated time; the 1×–8× buttons multiply how fast that clock runs, not how coarsely it is solved, so a burger cooked at 8× is the same burger.

    Tickets

    can carry up to three burgers with different orders. Form each one, then use the chips under the ticket, [ and ], or a click on a patty to choose which one the buttons act on. They all go out together, so a burger that rests too long while the others cook goes out lukewarm. A cold patty pulls the pan down under it.

    Extras

    the buttons under Extras put toppings in the pan beside the patties: two bun halves cut side down, a rasher of bacon, an egg, a heap of sliced onions. They are cooked, not conjured: each one takes a spot on the metal, drags the rings under it down, boils its own water off, and browns (or burns) on its own kinetics. Select one from the chips or by clicking it, and Flip turns it over — or stirs the onions, which is the only way to caramelise all of them instead of scorching the layer on the pan. Off the heat takes it to the pass. A bun face goes golden in about a minute over 200 °C metal and black in three; bacon needs eight minutes at 180 °C to render 50–65 % of its fat and crisp, and comes off at a little under half its raw weight; a lid or a flip is what sets an egg yolk — about eighty seconds under the lid, or thirty after a flip, and the jammy window is only a few seconds wide — and a fried egg loses 10–15 % of its mass, not a third; onions want a quarter of an hour on medium — with the water out of them but no colour on them they are sweated, which is soft and mild, not raw. If the pan is too crowded a topping goes down half on top of something else, and only the part touching the metal cooks. Rest the food, open Build, and place each layer yourself. Fresh toppings and sauces go straight into the stack. For a double, form a second patty, cook it, then add it from the pass. Undo layer removes the last addition; Unpack returns cooked food to the pass for reheating. Close with the matching top bun, then cut and serve. Only food you placed counts toward the order.

    Charcoal

    pick the kettle as the stove (with nothing on the heat yet: it swaps the pan for a cold grate). The knob is the bottom vent: open it, wait for the bed to glow and the grate to pass 250 °C. The bars brand marks, radiant heat browns the edges, fat that drips on the coals flares up and soots the meat, and the lid turns the kettle into an oven. Over bars there is no pan: pressing only squeezes juice and fat onto the fire — nothing to smash against — cheese that hangs past the meat sags through the grate in flaming gobs unless you put it on late and drop the lid, and the wash button becomes a wire brush that takes the residue off without cooling the bars. Bank coals rakes the bed to one side: the same charcoal, twice as deep over half the grate and bare ash under the other half, so the bars run 180–230 °C cooler over there and a patty sees about a sixth of the radiant heat. The split is half there after a couple of minutes and takes eight to ten to settle, so bank it early — you can sear over the pile while it finds the rest of it. Sear over the coals, then drag it across to finish — that is how you get a crust on a thick patty without charring it.

    The fire is a thing you keep

    the kettle carries on between tickets exactly as the pan does: the coals go on burning down, ash goes on building, and the grate keeps its heat. Add coals tips in more lump straight from the bag; it is cold, so it takes heat out of the bed for the first few minutes and only then lifts it — add coals before you need them, not when the fire is already dying. Ash is not decoration: it falls through the bed, buries the bottom vent and strangles the draught, so an unswept bed runs cooler and burns slower at the same vent setting. Stir rakes it through — the ash drops into the bowl, the fire glows up for a minute or two — and Empty ash tips the bowl out, which you can only do once the kettle is cold.

    Wood and smoke

    Add wood puts a 60 g chunk of hickory, apple or mesquite on the coals. Nothing happens for the first three minutes while the bed boils the water out of it and takes it to about 300 °C, and then it smoulders for fifteen to seventeen: the smoke rate peaks once the whole chunk is alight and fades as it is eaten away. Smoke lands on the meat at a couple of millimetres a second, faster while the surface is still cool and wet, so it goes in early — a moderate deposit is a real flavour and is worth up to three of the twenty crust marks. Mesquite is half again as strong as hickory; apple is hard to overdo. With air the smoke is thin and blue: the volatiles burn as they leave the wood. Smothered — lid on and both vents shut — they never ignite, come off cold and white, and condense on the meat as creosote, which is bitter and costs you up to six. The lid vent is the other half of the airflow when the lid is on (the two vents are orifices in series, so the smaller one rules) and it is also how the smoke leaves: shut it and the smoke stops moving over the food and starts sitting on it.

    Moving things

    left-drag a patty (or a topping) to slide it across the pan or the grate; the ring shows where it will land, green if it fits and red if something is in the way. Dragging from anywhere else still orbits the camera. Move to centre and Move to edge do the same from the keyboard. Position is not decoration: the metal under it, the burner's hot spot and — on a banked kettle — which side of the fire it is on all follow it, and so does the heat it pulls out of the pan. Meat welds itself to hot metal until its crust sets, so moving it too early tears the face off exactly as an early flip does; Scrape (S) works the blade under it first, which costs a second of searing and about 40 % of the tearing.

    The cook's senses

    under Senses, and the only instruments you have in hard mode, where the probe, the IR gun and the Inspector are gone, the log's temperatures read ·· °C, and the results card names the doneness the burger came out as without giving you the number or the temperature traces. Press test (T) puts a finger on the middle of the patty: what you feel is the compression modulus, computed from the myosin, collagen and actin that have denatured through the thickness and how dry each layer is. The layers are springs in series, so the softest one — the centre — dominates, which is exactly why the test works. Raw is slack, rare is soft, medium-rare springs back, medium is firm with give, well done is hard. It costs about a tenth of the juice a spatula press does, and a second and a bit with your hand over a hot pan. Peek (K) cuts into it: you see the colour at the centre and how deep the grey band has come in, the viewport shows you the actual cut for a few seconds, and then you have a burger with a slit in it that weeps out of the cut for the rest of the cook and costs you a quarter of the structure mark per cut (three cuts is as bad as it gets). It can never score 100 again. Hand over it (H) holds a palm about 8 cm over the metal and counts: radiant plus convective flux at the hand, through the Stoll burn curve. Over coals it is the chart everybody learns: two seconds is searing, four is very hot, six is hot, eight or more is only medium. A pan is not a fire, so the same count means a far hotter surface and the words are on a pan scale: about twenty seconds is a 200 °C pan — the recipe temperature — thirteen is 250 °C, nine is a 300 °C pan past any oil's smoke point. Over a banked kettle with nothing on it the hand goes over each side in turn.

    Listen to it.

    The sizzle is a reading, not decoration. A wet face against the metal is a loud, rough, low crackle — water flashing at the contact — and nothing browns while you can hear it. When it drops to a quiet, much higher hiss with sparse pops, that face has boiled dry and Maillard has started: that is your cue to expect a crust, and the log writes it down for you (“the sizzle has dropped to a hiss”). A patty lifted on the blade goes quiet — but a pan is never silent just because there is no meat in it: rashers rendering, an egg in the fat and onions boiling all speak in the same two voices. A kettle roars as the vents open and whooshes when fat flares on the coals, and a lid low-passes the lot.

    The shift

    is six tickets. Each one is quoted a time from the moment you say “yes chef” — about 1.6× the recipe for what was ordered, so one medium-rare is 13:10 and a three-top is 20:00 to 24:00 depending on what is on it — and the HUD shows the clock against it. The clock runs in kitchen time, so the speed buttons do not buy you service time. Going over costs up to 10 ticket points at twice the quote and the table mentions it. A ticket with a plate sent back scores nothing, and service moves on.

    The customer

    gets the last word. Above the bars they say what they actually think — dry, no crust, burnt, cold in the middle, under, over — and the plate is sent back (under 45, or anything raw or burnt on it), accepted, or delighted (90+). They tip on the bill: nothing on a plate that goes back, ~10 % on a mediocre one, 20 % on a good one, 25 % on a perfect one, and less if it was late or lukewarm. At the end of the six you get the night's card, and your best shift is remembered in this browser.

    Doneness

    is the peak centre temperature, including carry-over while resting. Rare 49–52 °C, medium-rare 54–57 °C, medium 60–63 °C, medium-well 65–68 °C, well done 71+ °C.

    Keys

    space lay in / flip, F flip, P press, S scrape, C cutaway, T press test, K peek, H hand over the pan, [ and ] select the next patty or topping, 1–4 view presets, R reset the camera.

    Tips from real life

    preheat properly; don't press a thick patty; a thumb dimple stops it doming; flipping often cooks faster and more evenly; the juice pooling on top is a flip cue; meat releases from the pan on its own once the crust sets; rest before cutting.
